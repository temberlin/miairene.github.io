FIRE, WATER, MOLD, RUST: thatcherblackwood video process


This covers some of the process in making my section of the video, but does not explain the story. More info on the story can be found in the other songs & visuals :). There’s also a lot of little details I’ve left you to find for yourself, be sure to check out the horizontal version especially. link

FUMAGE

Holding a piece of paper above a candle flame at an angle to capture the soot, I created these wispy shapes that, with the colors inverted and altered, looked like flames. I like the idea of reality and its representation having an indirect but indexical relationship; the poetry of making digital flames out of physical soot.


ARSON

I had my friend Ember Streshinky build a miniature of the house and we set it on fire. She is an excellent scenic designer who took care to make the building appropriate to the story, cutting out itsy bitsy planks for the siding and panels for the roof. We took it to a spot that was 100% concrete all-around with a fire extinguisher and a bucket of water. Holding the quivering flame of the lighter to the back edge of the house, I was worried that with all of the effort that went into it, only one try at actually burning it, and the sun quickly setting, it would have all been one big waste of time, but after a couple of minutes of lighting little sections that died off, the flames started to climb up and around the miniature. I composited the miniature footage into an environment of archival imagery and animation, in love with the tornadoes of inferno twisting along the edges of the house and the rich color fall-off of flames’ glow. I’m looking forward to the next time I can do miniatures—and in the spirit of old-fashioned vfx, I’m also inspired to check out doing matte paintings!

ALTERNATIVE PRINTING PROCESSES

I really wanted to get chloroprinting (like this incredible work) to work for the stag fight, and tried out a bunch of different leaves, trying to find the right form of chlorophyll that bleaches in sunlight. Devastatingly, I was met with Absolutely No Success. If anybody has experience with this, especially in a similar climate to Southern California, let me know!

I ended up using cyanotype instead. I did rotoscope animation, printed the frames onto transparency, and exposed paper with ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide to sunlight for about 8 minutes. It then develops by soaking in water and drying.
The transparency is water soluble and can easily dissolve off if it gets wet, and I got this neat effect on one frame that accidentally got some water droplets on it. This might be interesting to explore further.

I’m happy with how this turned out. I’ve done cyanotype before, and it’s such a stunning process. The exposure starts off negative, inverts, and darkens; it feels like the paper itself is breathing. Also, the Prussian blue of cyanotype matches the color scheme of the video better than chloroprint would have anyways.

EBSYNTH

The saw shot is using EbSynth Beta, which is a style transfer software (this is the software but it looks like since I’ve downloaded it, they’ve changed things and made a paid version). I previously thought that this used some form of AI, but did a little more research, and Beta uses no neural networking at all. I asked my dad if he had any scary-looking hand saws, and he screwed up his face, “Hand saws do not look scary!” We ended up using a limb saw; the “scariest” one we could find. I recorded him brandishing the saw, then digitally painted over a handful of frames of the video. The software propagates the style across the rest of the video. I alternated between the different outputs every couple of frames, so the motion switches between fluid and more boil-like. I like using this software in ways where it is obvious I’m using it, rather than trying to disguise it as rotoscope animation. However, the more obvious approach looks more uncannily computerized, so it really depends on the project.

ORNAMNETATION

I’ve been fascinated by old school ornamentation recently, doing studies of filigree, rocaille, tiles designs, etc. The border for the pylon shot is a product of that fascination. I spent hours drawing in hundreds of laurel leaves to build the frame. The background footage is spare from basalt, it’s footage I took of a pylon projected onto a sheer curtain. I thought the results would be more interesting than they turned out, still looking for other ways to use projection!

WATERCOLOR COLLAGE

I started the deer stabbing shot during a kind of crazy windstorm, we lost power for almost a week and didn’t have internet, and thus no references. The horror! Not that I could have found any reference video of a deer stabbing another in the neck, especially at the angle I was imagining, but it would have been nice to not have been theorizing about the basics of deer anatomy. Locked in the basement hiding from the wind, I felt like one of those monks drawing cats with human faces and humans with cat faces in illuminated documents. What do deer knees look like? Where are their eyes in their head? This was punching a little above my weight animation-wise, but I think the end result is legible.

I printed out the frames I had drawn and painted the background in watercolor. Then, on a piece of wax paper overlaid, I sketched deer details. The idea was to create a bit of foggy texture pushing the background back, I think it was partially successful. The color of the deer is this archival image.

MEAT

This video was my first time purchasing raw red meat ever. I’m generally a casual vegan but I did eat it after using it for the video, I wouldn’t want to waste an animal like that. I put a layer of cling wrap down on the scanner and then scanned it multiple times while pulling it apart and cutting at it. Each frame then had to be cleaned up digitally to cut out the background and cling wrap. I did a similar thing cutting my way through a pomegranate. This was inspired by a shot I did pulling apart fabric for Ribbons ft. Saoirse Dream by Swanskin that turned out better than I expected, where I tore at embroidery of a braid until it was unrecognizable.

MATH

I’m a math major and it really bothers me when in media, for instance, there’s math on a blackboard of some supposed genius and it’s just…the quadratic formula or nonsense arithmetic. Obviously the equations in the video aren’t scientifically accurate because it’s fantasy, but they all do have to do with the topic at hand. The one topology class I took isn’t enough to understand theoretical quantum physics, but I did my best making it as legitimate as is reasonable. Some of the equations are also solvable and give you more info on the story! I don’t know if anyone will care enough to bother with calculus but I’ve always wanted to stick a math problem in a music video mwahaha >:)

ANIMATION

Amongst others, the shot of a character turning to face the camera is traditional animation. I recorded reference of myself and had to adjust for the proportions of the characters. I’m not awesome at likeness but hopefully the character who is also in the video footage is recognizable! I enjoyed sticking in some silly little smear frames--go through frame-by-frame and maybe you'll find a two-headed deer.

BURNING

I digitally painted cloaked figures, printed and cut them out, and scanned in different stages of burning. The process of lighting and extinguishing without causing extra harm to the paper I found most effective was lighting the paper with a candle, then dropping it onto an upside down baking pan, which I covered with a right side up baking pan. This suffocated the flames effectively and delicately, and I was able to get very nice burnt edges. The printer inks shifted colors as they burned, which made for a beautiful effect but also reminds me that I probably should have been more careful about ventilation while doing this.

DIGITAL PAINTING

Obviously a lot of the video was digitally painted, but I’d like to highlight that the aspen leaves were painted in Heavypaint. It’s a free painting web app with a lot of weird tools to play with. I heard Vaughan Ling, the guy who made it, give a talk about the ethos of it and it’s pretty cool. I’d like to play around more with using this for painting backgrounds maybe? The rest is done in the oft accursed Photoshop.

STEREOGRAM

I made a 2.5D scene in After Effects and exported out two versions from cameras with slightly different locations horizontally, so the shot running through forest fire at least in theory works like a stereogram. The figure is more EbSynth, although it is significantly glitchier than the saw shot. There's dozens of aspen tree photo cutouts the make up the forest, and a handful of birch trees too, since they look similar enough and were easier to find. The background is this archival marbled flyleaf, which, coincidentally is from a book called “An Essay on Combustion.”

STROBE

I stole the idea for the strobe shot directly from a recent Charli video. (Not that she's the first to ever do it, but that's where I got the idea.) In messing around with this effect, I learned that the most important part is to have the basic shapes of luminance in each shot mix together nicely into a joint composition; if you have two random shots strobed together they muddle and lose definition. I don’t think that either composition is that strong on its own, but together this is one of my favorite shots of the video. I messed around a lot with the color of the video (using my own plugin! It’s name your own price feel free to pay zero dollars for it) because this is the only time I significantly use green. I feel just okay about the results but it’s better than where it started.

ARCHIVAL MATERIAL

I’ve been mentioning my use of archival material throughout this–I use it a lot–but the second to last shot is entirely archival material. It’s this crazy lace piece -- random aside: I SO want to learn how to make bobbin lace but that’s a whole other level of patience above even animation, embroidery, or chainmail, and I have absolutely no way to say that it’s for my career or anything “productive” (blah blah capitalism blah blah). It’s such an incredible craft. If you make bobbin lace or any other kind of lace let me know I want to hear about it! -- The lace is layered on top of a video from my own personal archive, an old project that never came to fruition. It’s a loop of smashed sugar glass.

MOLD

I printed out several versions of this frame, soaked them in sugary soymilk and left them in a dark warm place for a couple of weeks. I was pleasantly surprised with the variety of mold I got on the pages–vivid colors and bloomy, splashing shapes. I’d also like to experiment with different types of paper, how that might affect bleed, and ways I can stimulate microbiotic growth along the lines of a composition.

RUST

Taking a collection of old rusty things–pipes, blocks, nails, etc., I layered sections of old white sheets soaked in 50-50 white vinegar and water in between like a lasagna and wrapped loosely in a trash bag. You want to keep things damp but maintain airflow. Black plastic is good for absorbing heat. I left that for a couple of days under a window, and then retrieved the fabric, cleaned it in plain water, dried, and ironed. I’ve done rust printing in the past, and this was an improvement from before–I think because I had better rusty objects. I ended up really liking the darker parts that took a lot of pigment on, and in the future would probably push this further, positioning the fabric to have better contact with the rust.
Rust printing wasn’t the end of the process, though: after scanning them in, I cut out shapes of the darker rust and painstakingly placed them to form a face. I ended up with 133 layers of rust shapes plus 49 layers of cloud cutouts plus 82 shots of me blowing out a candle to make up the face and atmosphere. I pushed those layers into 3d space to create the motion for the final shot.

Working with thatcherblackwood is always a blast, and what a gauntlet the intensity of the song was! It's a privelege to be trusted to experiment with process in telling a story.
